Part I · Ground floor

How to use this guide

2 min read

Every research project starts as a vague interest and has to become one clear question you can answer with data in a few months. This guide walks you through that change, step by step, until you have a one-page research proposal your mentor can review. You don't need any background beyond curiosity and a willingness to rewrite your question several times.

What you'll be able to do when you finish

  • Tell a strong research question from a weak one, and explain why, using the FINER criteria and PICO.
  • Name the type of question you are asking (descriptive, comparative, relational, causal, predictive, or engineering) and the kind of study each type needs.
  • Turn a broad interest into a narrow, measurable question with operational definitions for every variable.
  • Write a testable hypothesis, or engineering goals with criteria when a hypothesis doesn't fit.
  • Choose a study design, spot confounding variables, and plan controls, randomization, and repeated trials.
  • Check that data exists and that you are allowed to use it before you commit.
  • Build a realistic 12 to 16 week timeline with milestones and backup plans.
  • Know which projects need review and approval before you start, and work well with a mentor.
  • Write a complete one-page research proposal.

How each chapter is built

Each chapter starts with the idea in plain words, then shows it with worked examples from several fields: biology, environmental science, computer science, psychology, and physics or engineering. Most chapters include a table you can come back to as a reference, and a few include sample writing you can model. Every chapter except this one and the glossary ends with a short Check yourself list. If you can answer those questions without looking back, move on.

Keep a running document called your question log. Every time a chapter asks you to try something (brainstorm topics, rewrite a question, define a variable), do it in that document. By the last chapter, the log will hold almost everything you need for your proposal.

A suggested pace

Week Chapters Goal
1 1 to 3 Know what makes a question strong and practice rewriting weak ones
2 4 to 6 Pick a topic, narrow it to one question, and write a hypothesis or goal
3 7 to 9 Choose a design, confirm your data source, and draft a timeline
4 10 to 12 Check rules and approvals, meet with your mentor, and finish your proposal

Some students move faster and some need five or six weeks, especially if the first idea doesn't survive the feasibility check in Chapter 8. That's normal. A question that dies in week 3 costs you far less than one that dies in week 10.

This guide is the Ask stage of Vector Research. If you haven't yet learned how to find and read papers, read "Finding and Reading Research Papers" alongside Chapters 4 and 5. When you reach the analysis plan, "Statistics for Research" covers the tests in depth.

What makes a research question good

6 min read

A research question is a single, precise question that your project is designed to answer. Everything else in a project (the data you collect, the methods you choose, the graphs on your poster) exists to answer it, so a fuzzy question produces a fuzzy project.

Five properties of a strong question

Researchers describe good questions in different ways, but most descriptions share five properties. Use these as a checklist every time you write or revise a question.

Property What it means Test yourself
Answerable Some combination of data and analysis could, in principle, settle it Can you describe the graph or table that would answer it?
Specific It names who or what, which variables, and where or when Would two people reading it plan the same study?
Measurable Every key term can be turned into a number or a category Can you say exactly how each variable will be measured?
Original enough It adds something: a new population, place, method, dataset, or combination Can you name the closest published study and how yours differs?
Worth answering Someone would care about the answer either way If the answer is "yes" or "no," does either result teach something?

Take a common starting point: "Does social media affect teenagers?" It fails almost every test. "Affect" could mean mood, sleep, grades, or friendships. "Social media" could mean any app, used in any way. There's no population, no time frame, and no measurement. Compare it with this version:

"Among 9th to 12th graders at one high school who complete an anonymous survey, is reporting 3 or more hours per school day on social media associated with reporting fewer than 8 hours of sleep on school nights?"

Now you can picture the answer. It would be a table comparing two groups, with the percentage of each group sleeping fewer than 8 hours. That ability to picture the final table or graph is the fastest test of whether a question is answerable. (A survey like this involves human participants, so it needs approval before you collect anything; see Chapter 10.)

How original is original enough

High school researchers often freeze because they think a question has to be completely new. It doesn't. Professional scientists rarely ask a question nobody has touched. They ask one that extends earlier work in a small, clear way. Your question is original enough if it does at least one of these:

  • Tests a known finding in a new population (a different age group, region, species, or soil type).
  • Uses a newer or larger dataset than earlier studies had.
  • Applies a different method to an old question, such as a machine learning model where earlier work used a simple regression.
  • Combines two datasets that nobody has linked before, such as air quality readings and school attendance.
  • Replicates an important study to see whether the result holds up.

You can only know where your question sits if you've read the closest published work. That's why reading papers comes before finalizing the question, and why Chapter 4 shows you how to mine papers for ideas.

The FINER criteria

Clinical researchers use a well-known checklist called FINER, described in the textbook Designing Clinical Research by Stephen Hulley, Steven Cummings, and colleagues. It appears in the chapter "Conceiving the research question and developing the study plan," which is Chapter 2 of the fourth edition (2013), written by Cummings, Warren Browner, and Hulley. The fifth edition was published in 2022. FINER says a good question is:

Letter Stands for What it asks Student version
F Feasible Can it be answered with the time, data, skills, and money available? Can I finish it in 3 to 6 months with free tools and public data or a small experiment?
I Interesting Does the answer interest you and others in the field? Will I still care about this in week 10? Would a judge ask follow-up questions?
N Novel Does it confirm, refute, or extend earlier findings, or provide new ones? What does my project add to the closest published study?
E Ethical Can it be done without unacceptable risk to participants, animals, or privacy? Does it need approval, and can I get approval in time?
R Relevant Could the answer change scientific knowledge, practice, policy, or future research? Who would use the answer, and how?

Although FINER came from clinical research, it works for any field. Feasibility is where most student projects fail, so give it the most weight. A brilliant question you cannot answer by your fair date is a worse choice than a modest question you can answer well.

A worked FINER check

Here's a student's first question and an honest FINER check.

First draft: "Can a new drug slow memory loss in people with early Alzheimer's disease?"

Criterion Verdict Why
Feasible Fails Testing a drug in patients requires a clinical trial, a medical team, years of follow-up, and approvals no student can get
Interesting Passes Very interesting to many people
Novel Unclear Depends on the drug
Ethical Fails for a student Giving drugs to patients is far outside what a student project can do safely
Relevant Passes The answer would matter

Revised: "In public gene expression data from brain tissue in NCBI GEO, which genes differ most in expression between people with and without Alzheimer's disease, and do those genes cluster in known biological pathways?"

The revision keeps the interesting, relevant core and moves it to a form a student can do with public, de-identified data, a laptop, and free software. Feasible and ethical now pass. Novelty depends on which dataset and which pathways, and reading the literature will tell you.

Questions that are too big, too small, or not research

Three kinds of questions show up again and again in early drafts.

  • Too big. "What causes cancer?" Whole careers and institutes work on this. Shrink the population, the variable, and the outcome until a few months of work could answer it.
  • Too small. "What is the boiling point of water at my house?" You can look it up or measure it in five minutes, and the answer teaches nothing new. Expand it into a pattern: how does boiling point change across a range of altitudes or dissolved salt concentrations, and does it match the theory?
  • Not research. "Should schools ban phones?" This is a values question. Data can inform it (does banning phones change test scores?), but data alone can't settle what people should do. Rewrite it into the empirical part you can measure.
Check yourselfAnswer out loud or on paper before moving on.
  1. Name the five properties of a strong research question and give a one-line test for each.
  2. What does each letter of FINER stand for, and which one sinks the most student projects?
  3. Give two ways a question can be original enough without being a world first.
  4. Rewrite "Is exercise good for you?" so that you can picture the final graph.
